# Analytical Chemistry

## Titration essentials
A titration finds the unknown concentration of an analyte by adding a standard
solution of exactly known concentration until the reaction is just complete.

- Standard solution: a reagent of accurately known concentration, prepared from
  a primary standard or standardised against one.
- Primary standard: high purity, stable in air, not hygroscopic, high molar mass
  so the weighing error stays small.
- Burette reading: read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level, to the nearest
  0.05 cm3; record both the initial and the final reading.
- Concordant titres agree to within 0.10 cm3. Use the mean of the concordant
  values and ignore any rough trial run.
- Always rinse the burette with the titrant and the pipette with the solution it
  will deliver, otherwise the solution is diluted and the result is wrong.

Q: Why is a rough titration carried out first?
A: To find the approximate end point quickly, so the accurate titrations can be
run by adding the titrant rapidly to within a few cm3 of the end point and then
dropwise. This keeps every accurate titre concordant and saves time.

## Acid-base indicators
An indicator is a weak acid whose conjugate base has a different colour, so the
colour change happens over the pH range where the two forms are both present.

- Phenolphthalein: colourless below pH 8.3, pink above pH 10, so it suits a
  strong base titrated against a weak acid.
- Methyl orange: red below pH 3.1, yellow above pH 4.4, so it suits a strong
  acid titrated against a weak base.
- The end point must sit on the steep part of the pH curve; for a strong acid
  with a strong base almost any indicator works.

## Redox titrations
- Potassium manganate(VII) is its own indicator: the purple MnO4- ion is
  decolourised as it is reduced to almost colourless Mn2+.
- Acidify with dilute sulfuric acid, never with hydrochloric acid, because
  chloride ions are oxidised to chlorine and the titre comes out too high.
- Iodine-thiosulfate titration: starch is added near the end point, when the
  solution is pale straw coloured, because starch-iodine complex held too long
  does not break down cleanly.

## Worked calculation
25.00 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm-3 sodium hydroxide is neutralised by 23.40 cm3 of
hydrochloric acid.

- Moles of NaOH = 0.100 x 0.02500 = 2.50 x 10-3 mol.
- The equation is 1:1, so the acid also supplies 2.50 x 10-3 mol.
- Concentration of the acid = 2.50 x 10-3 / 0.02340 = 0.107 mol dm-3.
